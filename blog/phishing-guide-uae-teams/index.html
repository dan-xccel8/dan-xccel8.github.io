Phishing is getting harder to spot: a practical guide for UAE teams

X XCCEL8 Team
17 October 2026·6 min read

The phishing email of ten years ago was almost a caricature: broken English, a generic “Dear Customer”, a prince offering you money. Most office workers learned to spot those in seconds — and that training worked, for a while.

It does not work anymore. Today’s phishing messages are written in fluent, natural language, address you by name, reference your actual suppliers, and arrive at exactly the right moment. Generative AI has removed the two most reliable warning signs people were ever trained on — poor grammar and generic messaging — and the result is lures that are nearly indistinguishable from legitimate mail. This is why phishing remains one of the most common ways attackers get into businesses, year after year.

For UAE teams, where business moves fast across email, WhatsApp and SMS, the exposure is constant. Here is a practical look at how phishing works in 2026, why people fall for it, and what actually stops it.

The old tells are gone

Most security awareness training from the last decade taught staff to look for clumsy language and impersonal greetings. Attackers have adapted. AI-crafted phishing now reads perfectly, adopts the tone of the impersonated colleague or vendor, and personalises the message with details scraped from public sources. The “obvious fake” is increasingly the exception, not the rule — so a team trained only on the old signs is effectively unprotected against the new ones.

New lures: QR codes, voice calls and text messages

Phishing has also diversified beyond email. QR-code phishing — sometimes called “quishing” — accounts for roughly 12% of phishing incidents in some analyses: a code on a poster, in a PDF attachment, or even inside an otherwise innocent email sends the victim’s phone to a fake login page, neatly bypassing email scanners that never inspect the image. Voice phishing (“vishing”) uses AI-cloned voices to impersonate executives or IT support on a phone call, while SMS phishing (“smishing”) exploits the urgency people feel about a text. The 2026 Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report — which covered 31,000+ security incidents and 22,000+ confirmed breaches across 145 countries — found that multiple studies attribute over 36% of breaches to phishing as the initial access vector, and in some studies phishing is involved in around 90% of successful cyberattacks. The channel keeps changing; the tactic stays the same.

What actually gets people: urgency, authority, routine

Phishing works because it exploits how people behave at work, not because of technical trickery. Three patterns account for most successful lures:

  • Urgency. “Process this payment before 4pm or the account is frozen.” A rushed person skips verification.
  • Authority. A message that looks like it comes from the CEO, a government entity or a bank. People hesitate to question authority.
  • Routine. The most effective lures of all are boring: a fake supplier invoice that looks like the hundred real ones you paid last month, a “password expiry” notice that arrives on a Monday morning. Attackers study business processes precisely so their emails blend into the day’s ordinary traffic.

It is worth remembering that phishing rarely acts alone. The 2026 DBIR found that exploitation of vulnerabilities has overtaken credential abuse for the first time as the leading initial access vector at 31% — but stolen credentials harvested through phishing remain a close and closely related second act. And the stakes are high: ransomware appeared in 48% of breaches in the 2026 report, with a median ransom paid of $139,875 — while 69% of victims refused to pay. Separately, third parties were involved in 48% of breaches, which is why a fake invoice from a “supplier” can be so damaging. For smaller businesses the exposure is real: 96% of ransomware victims, where organisation size was known, were SMBs.

The practical defence stack

No single tool stops phishing. What works is a layered stack, and none of these layers is expensive or exotic:

  1. Email filtering that actually inspects. A modern secure email gateway blocks most malicious mail before it reaches inboxes — including AI-crafted lures and malicious attachments. This is your first and cheapest line of defence.
  2. Multi-factor authentication everywhere. MFA does not stop someone clicking, but it stops the click from becoming a breach: stolen credentials alone are not enough when a second factor is required. Enforce it on email, remote access, cloud apps and admin accounts — no exceptions for executives.
  3. Staff training with simulated phishing. A yearly awareness video changes nothing. Short, realistic simulated phishing campaigns — run quarterly, with immediate feedback when someone clicks — measurably reduce click rates over time. The goal is instinct, not memory: staff who have practised spotting lures react faster.
  4. A simple “report it” culture. Every inbox needs a one-click way to report suspicious messages, and — critically — the person who reports must never be punished for it. A team that reports quickly contains incidents; a team that hides its mistakes lets them spread. Celebrate the catches, not just the clicks.

The first hour after someone clicks

Someone will click eventually. What happens in the next hour decides whether it is an anecdote or an incident:

  • Disconnect the device from the network — unplug the cable or switch off Wi-Fi. This stops malware from spreading or data from leaving.
  • Do not shut it down. A powered-off machine destroys useful forensic evidence; disconnect it but leave it running.
  • Reset the credentials involved — the entered password and anything similar — from a clean device, and revoke active sessions.
  • Call your IT team or security provider immediately with exactly what happened: what was clicked, what was entered, and when. Speed beats embarrassment every time.

Write this procedure down, keep it to one page, and make sure everyone knows where to find it. An untested plan is barely better than no plan.
